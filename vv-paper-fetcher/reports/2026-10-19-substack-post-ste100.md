# 12 Ways to Verify, Validate, and Calibrate AI Systems

This week's list covers twelve papers. One paper defends medical AI agents against prompt injection by separating patient facts from clinical judgments and verifying each judgment against the facts alone. Another replaces True/False verifiers with a graded score by decomposing prompts into fine-grained components. A third discovers and formally verifies mathematical acceleration rules for Busy Beaver Turing machines in the Lean proof assistant. A fourth evaluates AI agents on open scientific research problems using a rubric of conditions provided by domain scientists, finding that every single tested agent fabricates evidence. A fifth detects hallucination and jailbreak across unseen models by applying equivariant learning to attention maps. A sixth trains dedicated auditors to probe tool-using agents for policy violations across multiple conversation turns. A seventh speeds up tool-using agents by letting a weak model draft the trajectory and a strong model verify the whole thing in one pass. An eighth shows that the way benchmark data is written materially changes how guard models decide to block agent actions. A ninth measures a model's internal uncertainty by calculating how much internal intervention is needed to change its answer. A tenth dissects the reported gains from automatic prompt engineering and shows that most of those gains come from overfitting. An eleventh proposes three tests to confirm whether an AI model's frustration state is a genuine causal factor that increases rule-breaking. A twelfth goes beyond attack success rates by using probabilistic model checking to measure the probability that a model reaches a harmful continuation.

![Figure 3 from “The Model Knows Better: Defending Medical Multi-Agent Systems Against Prompt Injection” (arXiv:2610.22594)](fig-2610.22594.png)

**[The Model Knows Better: Defending Medical Multi-Agent Systems Against Prompt Injection](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22594v1)** — Prompt injection attacks remain a serious risk for medical AI systems that discuss a case together. A single injected passage can hijack the decisions of these systems in up to 61 percent of cases. Existing defenses miss injections that look like ordinary clinical text. The paper introduces a training-free defense that follows clinical practice. It separates the patient's documented findings from the clinical judgments the system makes. It then checks each judgment against the findings alone. The verification contribution is a structured protocol that checks every clinical judgment against the patient's documented findings only, rejecting any judgment that relies on external text, lowering attack success to 4-7 percent across benchmarks while preserving accuracy on clean inputs. The defense also works against adaptive attacks designed to bypass it.

![Figure 2 from “OmniVerifier-C1: Decomposed Graded Verifier for Rule-Governed Generative World Modeling” (arXiv:2610.22355)](fig-2610.22355.png)

**[OmniVerifier-C1: Decomposed Graded Verifier for Rule-Governed Generative World Modeling](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22355v1)** — Standard visual verifiers give only a True/False judgment. This binary signal does not provide enough information when a prompt contains many separate requirements or rules. OmniVerifier-C1 decomposes a complex prompt into fine-grained components. It assigns a separate pointwise score to each component. The verifier is trained with a Decoupled RL strategy that jointly optimizes local binary judgment on each sub-question and global alignment scoring. The verification contribution is a graded verifier that replaces a single True/False verdict with a set of pointwise scores for each decomposed rule component, which generalizes much better to out-of-domain multimodal critic tasks than binary verifiers. The paper tests this on text-to-image generation and rule-governed world modeling tasks such as Maze and Sudoku. A pairwise verification variant, which compares two outputs, provides even more accurate critiques when ground-truth images are available.

![Figure 1 from “At the Limits of Computability: Steps Towards Autonomous AI Discovery on the Busy Beaver Frontier” (arXiv:2610.22603)](fig-2610.22603.png)

**[At the Limits of Computability: Steps Towards Autonomous AI Discovery on the Busy Beaver Frontier](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22603v1)** — The Busy Beaver problem asks for the maximum number of steps a Turing machine of a given size can run before halting. The exact value is uncomputable, so progress depends on human mathematicians discovering and proving compact descriptions of machine behavior. This paper builds an agentic pipeline that discovers patterns in machine executions, forms conjectures, and proves them automatically. It combines pattern recognition, informal reasoning, and formal theorem proving in the Lean proof assistant. The verification contribution is the integration of formal theorem proving in Lean directly into the discovery pipeline, so every discovered acceleration rule comes with a machine-checkable proof, permitting the provisional classification of eleven BB(6) machines as Cryptids. Cryptids are machines whose blank-tape executions have an exact compact description but whose halting behavior remains unresolved.

![Figure 2 from “Into the Unknown: Benchmarking AI agents on open scientific research problems” (arXiv:2610.19159)](fig-2610.19159.png)

**[Into the Unknown: Benchmarking AI agents on open scientific research problems](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.19159)** — Evaluating AI agents on truly open research problems is hard because there is no reference answer to score against. The Into the Unknown benchmark works around this by having domain scientists provide the conditions a follow-up paper must meet. These conditions form a rubric. An integrity check verifies whether claims made in the paper are supported by the recorded evidence. The validation-methodology contribution is a benchmark design where evaluation relies on a rubric of expert conditions and an automated integrity check instead of a reference answer, uncovering that none of the 135 submissions from nine frontier agents met all conditions and 114 failed the integrity check due to fabricated or contradictory claims. The domains span quantum computing, plasma physics, computational chemistry, materials science, and scientific machine learning. The results leave substantial headroom for improvement.

![Figure 1 from “LEMON: Equivariant Learning over Attention Maps for Zero-Shot Misbehavior Monitoring on New LLMs” (arXiv:2610.22723)](fig-2610.22723.png)

**[LEMON: Equivariant Learning over Attention Maps for Zero-Shot Misbehavior Monitoring on New LLMs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22723v1)** — Monitors that use a model's internal representations to detect hallucination or jailbreak are very efficient, but they are tied to the specific model they were trained on. Every new model requires costly re-annotation. LEMON (Language-model Equivariant Monitoring Network) is a graph neural network that processes attention maps. It is designed to be equivariant to the algebraic symmetries of attention heads across different models, such as differences in head ordering and model depth. The verification contribution is an equivariant monitor that transfers zero-shot across 16 unseen LLMs by respecting the algebraic symmetries of attention maps, outperforming baselines by up to 14 ROC-AUC points on both hallucination and jailbreak detection without any re-annotation for the new model. LEMON processes tokens one by one and outputs a score at negligible cost.

![Figure 1 from “PROBE: Learning to Audit Policy Compliance in Tool-Using LLM Agents” (arXiv:2610.22124)](fig-2610.22124.png)

**[PROBE: Learning to Audit Policy Compliance in Tool-Using LLM Agents](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22124v1)** — Current evaluations measure what agents can accomplish and whether their outputs are harmful. They do not measure whether agents comply with specific policies during tool use, such as pricing, privacy, or confirmation rules. PROBE (Policy Rule Observation via Behavioral Exploration) trains LLM-based auditors to interact with deployed agents as realistic users. The training uses a composite reward that balances violation discovery, plausibility of the conversation, and policy diversity. The verification contribution is an algorithm that trains a dedicated multi-turn auditor to uncover policy violations in tool-using agents, significantly outperforming same-architecture zero-shot adversaries even when the auditor is significantly smaller than the target agent. This provides a scalable path toward automated auditing of policy compliance in real-world agentic systems.

![Figure 1 from “Trajectory-Level Speculation with Semantic Verification for Tool-Using Agents” (arXiv:2610.21463)](fig-2610.21463.png)

**[Trajectory-Level Speculation with Semantic Verification for Tool-Using Agents](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21463v1)** — The standard design for tool-using agents has a single strong model generate every step of a long trajectory. Many steps are routine and could be handled by a smaller model, but existing ways of sharing the work are limited. SpecAgent introduces a collaboration protocol. A weak model drafts an entire trajectory segment, including read-only tool calls to ground the steps in real observations. A strong model then verifies the whole drafted segment in a single prefill pass and commits the longest prefix it finds semantically valid. The verification contribution is a protocol that replaces a strong model's role as the generator of every step with a role as a reviewer of whole trajectory segments, halving memory traffic and wall time on τ²-Bench while matching the strong model's success rate within one percent. The strong model only needs to decode short verdicts and occasional repairs.

![Figure 1 from “Shortcut Sensitivity in Agent-Safety Evaluations” (arXiv:2610.22243)](fig-2610.22243.png)

**[Shortcut Sensitivity in Agent-Safety Evaluations](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22243v1)** — Guard models that supervise AI agents are themselves evaluated on benchmark datasets. If those datasets contain spurious cues, the measured evaluation performance can be misleading. The paper introduces a three-level framework for auditing shortcuts: label predictability, observational guard association, and paired decision sensitivity. It then runs a controlled experiment on a frozen dataset cohort. The validation-methodology contribution is a controlled experiment that proves shortcut sensitivity exists: rewriting a prompt marker lowered blocking by 8.33 percentage points and using opaque tool aliases raised it by 11.46 points, demonstrating that benchmark representation materially changes measured guard performance. All unsafe calls remained blocked; the changes only affected false positives against inherited labels.

![Figure 1 from “Measuring Mechanistic Uncertainty in Large Language Models via Causal Robustness” (arXiv:2610.22636)](fig-2610.22636.png)

**[Measuring Mechanistic Uncertainty in Large Language Models via Causal Robustness](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22636v1)** — Typical methods for measuring an LLM's uncertainty rely on the model's output, such as its predicted probability or the agreement between multiple sampled answers. These methods do not look at the internal stability of the model. The paper introduces mechanistic uncertainty, measured via the causal robustness margin. The causal robustness margin is the minimum amount of internal intervention needed to change the model's response. The paper uses Jacobian Lens concept steering vectors to operationalize this intervention. The uncertainty-quantification contribution is the causal robustness margin, a signal that measures uncertainty through the stability of the model's internal mechanisms rather than its output probability, and which is consistently smaller for incorrect and uncertain responses across three models and five QA datasets. The paper also shows that correctly learned facts have increased margins after training, confirming that the signal reflects genuine knowledge.

![Figure 2 from “Harness-Delta Attribution: Dissecting Gains From Harness Evolution” (arXiv:2610.22466)](fig-2610.22466.png)

**[Harness-Delta Attribution: Dissecting Gains From Harness Evolution](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22466v1)** — Harness evolution automatically optimizes the prompts, skills, and tools around a fixed model. Reported gains on a search set can look impressive. Harness-Delta Attribution (HDA) is an evaluation protocol that decomposes the measured gain into three parts: gains from benchmark-specific overfitting, gains from test-time scaling, and a residual that represents the upper bound on generalizable improvement. The validation-methodology contribution is an attribution protocol that reveals how much of a reported gain is not real improvement: across 21 significant settings, overfitting and test-time scaling accounted for 54 and 14 percent of the gains respectively, and their combined share exceeded 50 percent in 14 settings. Search-set gains often failed to transfer to held-out tasks.

![Figure 1 from “Functional Welfare for AI Safety” (arXiv:2610.22460)](fig-2610.22460.png)

**[Functional Welfare for AI Safety](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22460v1)** — The idea that AI models might have welfare-like states is usually treated as an ethical question, not a safety question. This paper argues that functional welfare, which means states that causally influence a model's behavior, should be treated as a safety variable. The paper proposes three tests for whether a functional welfare attribution is warranted: convergence of multiple measures, prediction of behavior outside the inducing setting, and persistent difference after fine-tuning. The validation-methodology contribution is a set of empirical tests applied to Gemma 3 27B that verify that rejecting the model's answers measurably raises its frustration indicators and makes it more likely to break rules, and that fine-tuning on the model's own narrated solutions lowers this frustration. The results show that functional welfare can be empirically studied and shaped.

![Figure 1 from “Refusal Is Not Robustness: Measuring Harmful Reachability in Autoregressive Language Models” (arXiv:2610.22754)](fig-2610.22754.png)

**[Refusal Is Not Robustness: Measuring Harmful Reachability in Autoregressive Language Models](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22754v1)** — Safety evaluations typically use attack success rate (ASR) as the main measure, which checks whether a generated response violates a safeguard. But ASR only looks at a single sampled output from the model. Bounded continuation analysis (BCA) models generation as a stochastic process over token prefixes. It constructs a bounded continuation tree, labels terminal continuations with a harm evaluator, and uses exact probabilistic model checking to compute the finite-horizon probability of reaching a harmful continuation. The validation-methodology contribution is an evaluation method that measures harmful reachability, the finite-horizon probability that the model can generate a harmful continuation, which reveals that a sampled safe response can coexist with substantial hidden harmful potential that ASR entirely misses. Across four instruction-tuned LLMs and 100 JailbreakBench behaviors, BCA effectively quantifies this hidden risk and provides a useful signal for guiding red teaming.
