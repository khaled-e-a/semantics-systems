# Fifteen Ways to Check If an AI System Is Correct

This week's list has fifteen papers. One paper uses a machine-checked proof system to let AI agents design real hardware. One paper fixes a statistics formula that other AI safety tools already depend on. One paper measures how often legal AI chatbots give wrong answers with high confidence. One paper builds and validates a grading system for an AI drug-discovery assistant. One paper shows that an AI judge can reach the right verdict through broken reasoning, and builds a way to catch this. One paper proves that certifying a model's predictions is not enough; a system must also certify its reasoning. One paper proves a mathematical safety guarantee that holds across a full multi-turn conversation, not just one reply. One paper gives a self-driving system a calibrated confidence range for its trajectory predictions, in a single computing pass. One paper proves a formal guarantee that a robot's actions stay correct even under a physical attack on its camera. One paper introduces a rule-writing language that lets a monitoring system check an autonomous system's likely next actions, not just its past ones. One paper turns an AI model's self-reported confidence in a math answer into a number that is actually calibrated. One paper tests whether failure-diagnosis tools for multi-step AI search agents find the real cause of an error, or just guess. One paper shows that the security review most companies use for AI agent "skills" does not measure whether the skill actually helps. One paper builds a method to catch AI judge panels that agree with each other for the wrong reasons. One paper adds checkpoints to a security task so a single pass-or-fail score cannot hide where an AI agent actually went wrong.

**[AI with Authority, from Application to Silicon](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21356v1)** — This paper asks a direct question: can an AI agent make real engineering decisions, from application code down to a physical chip, without a human checking every step? The paper answers with the Salt method. In the Salt method, AI agents exchange mathematical claims as proof artifacts, not as plain text. A proof artifact is a formal statement that a separate checking program can confirm is correct, without trusting the AI that wrote it. Each claim passes through a Lean 4 proof kernel, a small trusted program that checks mathematical proofs step by step. A single researcher used commercial AI agent subscriptions to drive a pipeline from application code, through a verified compiler, to a working RISC-V processor, in five weeks. An append-only log recorded 256 errors the system caught between July 7 and July 20, 2026. Zero incorrect proofs reached the final design. The verification contribution is this: the system treats machine-checked proof, not human review, as the condition for letting an AI agent act with real authority over code and hardware.

**[The Exceedance Design Effect: Effective Sample Size for Thresholds under Clustering](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21262v1)** — Many AI safety tools set a cutoff score from a calibration set, a batch of past examples used to pick the cutoff. Conformal prediction is one such tool. Conformal prediction gives a promise: a stated percentage of future predictions will fall inside a safe range. That promise assumes every calibration example is independent. In practice, many examples share a source, such as the same document or the same reasoning chain. Shared examples act like fewer independent data points than the raw count suggests. This paper derives a closed-form formula for the true effective count under this kind of clustering. The formula differs from a similar correction long used in survey statistics. The paper shows the correct effective count changes depending on where the cutoff is set, so a single dataset has no one true effective size. Testing on a released calibration set of 25,028 examples shows the standard correction can miss the true count in either direction. The uncertainty-quantification contribution is a corrected math foundation for a tool many AI safety systems already depend on.

**[Can Legal AI Know When It Is Wrong? And Do Students Know When It Is?](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21089v1)** — AI chatbots can state a wrong legal conclusion with high confidence. This paper measures how often that happens. The authors tested three AI chatbots on 60 contract-law cases. For each answer, the chatbot also gave a confidence score from 1 to 10. The paper defines a new measure: the High-Confidence Error Rate, or HCER. HCER counts how often a chatbot gives a wrong answer while stating 9 or 10 out of 10 confidence. One chatbot reached an HCER of 31.7 percent. The paper also surveyed 380 law students. Most students, 71.1 percent, had no formal training on ethical AI use. Even so, 81.6 percent understood the danger of an AI tool inventing fake case citations. The uncertainty-quantification contribution is HCER itself: a direct way to measure dangerous overconfidence in a high-stakes domain, paired with data on whether the humans using the tool can catch the errors.

**[Designing a Robust LLM-Based Evaluation System for Agentic AI in Drug Discovery Through Human Alignment](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21057v1)** — Standard text-matching scores, such as BLEU and ROUGE, cannot judge whether an AI drug-discovery assistant gave a scientifically sound answer. Human expert review can judge this, but experts cannot review every output fast enough. This paper builds a grading system for ChatInvent, an AI assistant used at AstraZeneca for drug discovery. The system grades answers on four dimensions: completeness, relevance, structural clarity, and whether the answer stayed within the question's scope. The authors compared four candidate AI judges against five human expert graders, to find which AI judge best matched expert judgment. They then improved the chosen judge's agreement with the human experts from 0.80 to 0.86, using a small set of example gradings the judge could learn from. The final system was tested on 70 held-out questions. The validation-methodology contribution is the human-alignment study itself: proof that an AI grading system's scores track real expert judgment, not just superficial answer style.

**[No Judgment Without a Reason: Counterfactual Receipts for Versioned AI Evaluators](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20938v1)** — When an AI system judges another AI's output, the judge can reach the right verdict through broken reasoning. Checking only the final verdict misses this problem. This paper introduces judgment receipts. A judgment receipt records the smallest change to the evidence that would flip a judge's verdict, so the receipt shows whether the reasoning actually held. The authors built ReasonBench, a benchmark of 19,520 test cases with verifiable reasoning chains. On the original, unaltered cases, a tested AI judge produced correct receipts 98.41 percent of the time. When the authors reworded the evidence without changing its meaning, correct receipt recovery fell to 54.8 percent. High accuracy on the plain task hid a severe weakness in the judge's actual reasoning. The validation-methodology contribution is direct: standard accuracy scores are not enough to trust an AI evaluator, and judgment receipts give a way to check the reasoning behind a verdict, not just the verdict itself.

**[Prediction certification cannot replace explanation certification](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20825v1)** — Many AI safety guarantees only certify a system's predictions: its accuracy, its calibration, or its conformal coverage. Calibration means a model's stated confidence matches how often it is actually right. This paper proves that prediction-only certification is not enough. The proof shows two models can score identically on every standard prediction metric, while behaving very differently once real-world conditions change. The authors introduce the competence envelope, a certification approach that requires evidence about both the prediction and the reasoning process behind it, not the prediction alone. Testing across several datasets and model types shows prediction-only certification misses failure modes that only appear once the reasoning process is examined directly. The uncertainty-quantification contribution is the proof itself, plus the competence envelope: a formal argument, backed by tests, that today's calibration and coverage guarantees are not sufficient on their own to call a model trustworthy.

**[Certified Multi-Turn Robustness for LLM Safety via Compositional Bounds and Safety Persistence](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20820v1)** — A jailbreak is a sequence of messages designed to make an AI system ignore its safety rules. Earlier certified-safety methods only covered a single message, and simply chaining that guarantee across a full conversation made the guaranteed safety level collapse fast. This paper introduces MTCR, a method that models a full conversation as a sequence of states with an adversary acting at each turn. MTCR proves a mathematical lower bound on safety that holds across many turns, not just one. A technique called safety persistence slows the decay of the safety bound from turn to turn, changing how fast the guarantee weakens as the conversation grows. The authors also prove a matching upper bound, showing their guarantee cannot be tightened further. Tests on six AI systems, against both fixed and adaptive multi-turn attacks, show real safety performance stayed above the proven guarantee in every case. The verification contribution is a certified, mathematically tight lower bound on AI safety across a full conversation, not just a single reply.

**[SPARC: Single-Pass Scaling for Motion Forecasting with Conformal Bayesian Last Layers](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20802v1)** — A self-driving system that predicts where other vehicles will move needs to know how confident it should be in that prediction. Older methods for this get a confidence estimate by running the same prediction many times with random variation, which costs computing time. This paper introduces SPARC, which produces a confidence estimate in a single pass, no repeated runs needed. SPARC adds a Bayesian last layer, a small statistical component that turns one forward pass through the model into a range of plausible outcomes instead of a single guess. SPARC then applies conformal calibration, which adjusts that range so it has a guaranteed 95 percent chance of containing the vehicle's real future path, regardless of how the model was trained. Across nine dataset and test setups, SPARC's predictions score better than comparable methods on standard accuracy and confidence measures. The uncertainty-quantification contribution is a calibrated, guaranteed-valid confidence range for a safety-relevant prediction, produced without the usual computing cost.

**[CertVLA: Certified Defense against Physical Visual Attacks for Vision-Language-Action Models](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20791v1)** — A vision-language-action model is an AI system that reads a camera image and a text instruction, then outputs a robot action. An attacker can place a physical patch or pattern in the camera's view to make the robot act wrongly. Earlier certified defenses only covered simple image-labeling tasks, not the continuous, moment-to-moment actions a robot takes. This paper introduces CertVLA. CertVLA covers parts of the camera image with several different masks, then only accepts an action if the model gives a consistent answer under every mask. CertVLA combines the results across a full robot task to produce a formal, proven guarantee: against any attacker limited to a bounded patch size, every action CertVLA approves matches what the model would have done with no attack present. The authors tested CertVLA both in simulation and on a real robot, against physical patch and texture attacks. The verification contribution is a proven, mathematical guarantee of correct robot behavior under physical attack, not just an empirical defense that happens to work in tests.

**[Runtime Verification under Split Past and Future](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20783v1)** — Runtime verification checks a running system against a rulebook of required behavior while the system operates. Older runtime verification tools only check what a system has already done. An autonomous system also needs assurance about what it is about to do next. This paper introduces Split Linear Temporal Logic, a rule-writing language that lets one rule cover both the past and the future in a single specification. The rule's past part reads the system's history to decide which requirements currently apply. The rule's future part checks several predicted next steps against those requirements and rules out any predicted step that would break a rule. The authors build a monitoring system that evaluates both parts together, in real time, as the system runs. The verification contribution is a new formal method for giving an autonomous system live assurance about upcoming, not-yet-taken actions, not only about actions already completed.

**[DirEAG: Dirichlet Evidence Aggregation for Calibrating Verbalized Confidence in Mathematical Reasoning](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20717v1)** — When an AI system solves a math problem, it can also state a confidence score in plain language, such as saying it is 80 percent sure of its answer. That stated number often does not mean what it claims. Asking the same model the same question with different prompt wording shifts its confidence scores, even when the underlying answer stays the same. This paper introduces DirEAG. DirEAG treats each stated confidence score as a piece of weighted evidence about which candidate answer is correct, including an explicit option for the case where none of the candidate answers is right. DirEAG then combines this evidence across several prompt variations into one calibrated confidence number. Tested on three math benchmarks with three different AI models, DirEAG produced better-calibrated confidence scores than simply averaging the raw stated numbers, while keeping accuracy competitive. The uncertainty-quantification contribution is a new mechanism for turning an AI system's self-reported, often unreliable confidence into a number that actually tracks real correctness.

**[When Failures Propagate: Causal Failure Attribution in Agentic Retrieval-Augmented Generation](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20627v1)** — An agentic retrieval system answers a question by searching for information across several steps, then reasoning over what it found. An error at an early search step can stay hidden until a much later step, and a later search can sometimes correct an earlier mistake. This makes it hard to know which step actually caused a wrong final answer. This paper builds AgenticRAG-FP, a benchmark that tests failure-diagnosis tools directly. The authors deliberately insert a known error at a chosen step, let the system continue running, and then check whether a diagnosis tool correctly names the step where the error was inserted. On three-step questions, one diagnosis method correctly found the error 91 percent of the time when the error was at the first step, but its accuracy fell to zero for errors at later steps. A second method, based on testing alternate versions of the evidence, did better on later-step errors. The validation-methodology contribution is the deliberate-error testing method itself: a way to check whether a failure-diagnosis tool actually finds the true cause, rather than trusting the tool's output on faith.

**[Evaluating Skills, Not Just Agents: Agentic Continuous Evaluation of Skills](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20614v1)** — Companies now give AI agents packaged "skills," reusable instructions and tools for a specific task. Before deployment, these skill packages usually pass through a review that checks their code structure and security, not whether the skill actually helps the agent complete real work. This paper introduces ACES, a testing method that runs the same task twice, once with a skill and once without, then measures the real difference in outcome. ACES calls this difference Skill Lift. Tested on 145 real skills from company and public sources, the authors found that the standard structural review score barely relates to Skill Lift, a correlation of only 0.14 out of a possible 1.0. Across 947 paired test runs on 58 skills used in production, the average Skill Lift was 0.2134, and 72.8 percent of tested skills showed a positive lift. The validation-methodology contribution is exposing a real gap: the review gate most companies already use does not measure whether a skill actually works.

**[JuryProbe: An Empirical Consensus-Risk Diagnostic for Routing Reference-Free Factuality Judge Panels to Grounded Verification](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20607v1)** — A panel of AI judges can check whether a claim is factually true without being given a reference document to check against. If every judge in the panel shares the same blind spot, their agreement looks like confidence, but is not real evidence the claim is true. This paper introduces JuryProbe, a diagnostic that estimates this hidden shared-blind-spot risk from a small labeled test set. When JuryProbe flags a panel as high risk, the flagged cases get sent to the same judges again, this time given a grounding reference document to check against. On a test set of altered claims, judges without a reference showed strongly correlated blind spots, and adding the reference document removed every case of false agreement the authors checked, 34 out of 34. A fixed routing rule caught real risk in 8 to 10 out of 10 real test batches, versus 0 out of 10 on a control batch with no real risk present. The verification contribution is a practical routing method: send only the risky, low-confidence cases to costly grounded verification, and let the rest pass on cheap panel agreement.

**[Beyond End-to-End Success: Diagnosing Failures in Long-Horizon Security LLM Agents](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20563v1)** — A long security task can have many steps, and a simple pass-or-fail score at the end does not show where an AI agent actually went wrong. An agent can fail before it ever reaches the step that tests the skill a researcher wanted to measure. This paper builds a diagnostic method that adds checkpoints inside a security task. The checkpoints separate failures that happen before an agent reaches a target skill from failures that happen after. The authors then test suspected causes directly, by changing one factor at a time and checking the effect. In one task, checkpoint analysis on an AI model showed many failures happened before the model even noticed information it was later expected to reuse. Giving the model clearer task instructions raised its success rate on noticing that information from 65.5 percent to 95.4 percent. Repeating the exact same test on a newer version of the model reversed the finding, and the same fix no longer predicted success at all. The validation-methodology contribution is the checkpoint-and-intervention protocol itself: proof that a single end-to-end success score can hide exactly where, and why, an agent fails, and that the answer can shift between model versions.
