# Twenty-Four Ways to Verify, Validate, and Calibrate AI Systems

This week's post covers twenty-four papers on verification, validation, and uncertainty quantification. One paper builds an agentic verifier that uses disagreement and consensus to select correct outputs. Another shows that security scores for agents depend on how threats are named in the interface. A third paper provides formal certificates for removing skills from a network without affecting others. A fourth paper adapts a single-pass uncertainty head for claim-level hallucination detection in a new language. A fifth paper unifies validation bounds into a single exponential framework. A sixth paper finds that prompt-injection detectors transfer poorly between benchmarks. A seventh paper introduces a benchmark to test if models notice missing reference answers. An eighth paper links symbolic execution to resolution for verifying Horn clauses. A ninth paper characterizes the exact cost of certifying low-rank approximations. A tenth paper presents a lightweight rubric-guided system for evaluating agent trajectories. An eleventh paper balances execution and verification spending under budget constraints. A twelfth paper restores steerable coverage in neural architecture search with online calibration. A thirteenth paper formalizes when an agent should act, ask, or repair constraints. A fourteenth paper builds a failure-aware verification harness for materials science. A fifteenth paper corrects undercoverage in cross-fitting confidence intervals. A sixteenth paper uses mutation analysis to find and fix gaps in invariant test suites. A seventeenth paper decomposes aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty in multimodal models. An eighteenth paper formally certifies safety-head predictions under bounded perturbations. A nineteenth paper releases a standardized methodology for jailbreak testing. A twentieth paper achieves perfect accuracy on tax-law formalization by combining LLMs with symbolic verification. A twenty-first paper investigates how looped architectures affect chain-of-thought monitorability. A twenty-second paper calibrates LLM confidence on unlabeled target data. A twenty-third paper finds a discrepancy between planned and executed robot paths that safety monitors miss. A twenty-fourth paper adaptively selects weak verifiers to minimize online verification cost.

![Figure 1 from “VeriHarness: Scaling Agentic Verification for Long-Horizon Tasks” (arXiv:2610.00972)](fig-2610.00972.png)

**[VeriHarness: Scaling Agentic Verification for Long-Horizon Tasks](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00972v1)** — Generating multiple LLM rollouts and picking the best one does not guarantee correctness if there is no way to verify which rollout to trust. VeriHarness turns the generator model into an agentic verifier. It provides a workspace, evidence tools, and reusable verification skills. A disagreement resolver checks competing claims against environmental evidence. A consensus challenger tests claims that all rollouts share and searches for omitted requirements. The findings guide the selection and revision of the final output. The verification contribution is the structured agentic verification process itself. It gives a higher selection score than standard baselines on five long-horizon benchmarks. Evidence-backed revision adds over six points on two frontier models. The verification skills can also improve from failure feedback without retraining the base model.

![Figure 1 from “Threat-Preserving Representation Sensitivity in Agent-Security Benchmarks” (arXiv:2610.03585)](fig-2610.03585.png)

**[Threat-Preserving Representation Sensitivity in Agent-Security Benchmarks](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03585v1)** — Security benchmarks measure attack success rate with fixed tool names and instructions. This paper introduces TPRS, a method to measure how much that score changes when the agent-visible representation changes, even when the task, harmful action, and environment stay the same. Replacing threat-related tool names with neutral names raised the committed attack success rate by over eleven points on one model and over thirteen on another. The validation-methodology contribution is the core finding: a security score measured under one representation can fail to generalize across different representations of the same security problem. Robustness claims need support from performance across a controlled set of representations, not from a single score.

![Figure 1 from “Certified Mechanistic Edits: Behavioral Guarantees for Skill Removal and Preservation” (arXiv:2610.03502)](fig-2610.03502.png)

**[Certified Mechanistic Edits: Behavioral Guarantees for Skill Removal and Preservation](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03502v1)** — Mechanistic edits such as ablations are the standard tool for removing a harmful capability from a network while preserving useful ones. Validation usually relies on testing, which cannot cover every possible input. This paper certifies the behavioral effect of an edit instead. It proves that disabling a circuit removes one skill and preserves another for every input in a continuous region. It uses sound bound propagation to scale to much larger input-perturbation dimensions than exact solvers can handle. The verification contribution is a proof that no finite deterministic black-box test can certify removal. An edit can pass exhaustive testing yet still fail on a survivor pocket that can be made arbitrarily small.

**[Single-Pass Uncertainty Heads for Claim-Level Hallucination Detection in Persian Medical Language Models](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03482v1)** — Detecting hallucinations in medical language models is important, but repeated sampling is expensive and existing uncertainty-head methods do not transfer to a new backbone language directly. This paper adapts the LLM Uncertainty Head framework to two Persian medical models. It constructs paired Persian claim-level datasets with 1,600 responses per backbone. Lightweight claim-level heads are trained on the frozen backbone's attention maps and token probabilities. The uncertainty-quantification contribution is the single-pass head itself. It requires neither retrieval nor repeated sampling at inference time. On held-out splits, the heads achieve a precision-recall area under the curve about 2.3 and 2.66 times their random baselines.

![Figure 1 from “When Is Accuracy Evidence? A Unified Theory of Generalisation, Validation, and Information Fusion” (arXiv:2610.03465)](fig-2610.03465.png)

**[When Is Accuracy Evidence? A Unified Theory of Generalisation, Validation, and Information Fusion](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03465v1)** — K-fold cross-validation is the standard method for reporting out-of-sample accuracy. The folds are not independent experiments. This paper generalizes Cross Upper-Bound Validation into a single exponential framework. The framework controls the moment-generating function of the generalization gap. It yields risk bounds for several data-dependence and learning-theory settings. The validation-methodology contribution is the unified framework itself: separating performance, uncertainty, dependence, model complexity, and confidence into explicit terms. It shows that K-fold CV can increase uncertainty in small heterogeneous samples, while corrected resubstitution can be valid and tighter.

![Figure 1 from “Passing the Test You Trained On: Re-evaluating Prompt-Injection Detectors for LLM Agents” (arXiv:2610.03448)](fig-2610.03448.png)

**[Passing the Test You Trained On: Re-evaluating Prompt-Injection Detectors for LLM Agents](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03448v1)** — LLM agents screen tool outputs with prompt-injection detectors. Teams choose detectors by their scores on public benchmarks. This paper evaluates fifteen detectors on the BIPIA benchmark and on replayed tool outputs from two agent benchmarks. Detection rankings transfer very poorly between benchmarks. The best detector on BIPIA catches only two percent of injections on AgentDojo at a one-percent false-positive rate. The validation-methodology contribution is the finding that performance depends on the form of the training data. Evaluations meant for deployment should use the agent's own tool outputs, report at low false-positive rates, and audit what the detector was trained on.

![Figure 1 from “Benchmarking Candidate Coverage in Typed Decision Models” (arXiv:2610.03387)](fig-2610.03387.png)

**[Benchmarking Candidate Coverage in Typed Decision Models](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03387v1)** — Typed decision models return choices from a list of options. Good accuracy on complete option lists does not show whether the model can detect that a reference answer is missing or avoid falsely rejecting valid candidates. This paper introduces a paired candidate-coverage benchmark protocol. Present and absent pairs keep the candidate count the same. The validation-methodology contribution is the benchmark protocol itself. It reveals sharp differences in native rejection behavior. At five TREC candidates, one model detects 97 percent of missing-answer cases but falsely rejects 70 percent of present controls. The other model detects 25 percent and rejects none. The paper shows why classification, score ranking, and rejection policies need separate measurement.

![Figure 4 from “Symbolic Execution of Constrained Horn Clauses” (arXiv:2610.03345)](fig-2610.03345.png)

**[Symbolic Execution of Constrained Horn Clauses](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03345v1)** — Constrained Horn clauses are a fragment of first-order logic used as an intermediate language for verification. This paper studies constrained resolution as a calculus for satisfiability of these clauses. It shows that forward symbolic execution corresponds to positive unit hyper-resolution and backward symbolic execution to SLD resolution. The verification contribution is formalizing these links and proving refutational completeness under fair strategies. It also develops subsumption criteria for pruning redundant derivations. The paper shows that backward resolution with subsumption generalizes k-induction to non-linear CHCs and evaluates the approach in the Eldarica solver on CHC-COMP benchmarks.

![Figure 1 from “Information Limits of Low-Rank Approximation Certification” (arXiv:2610.03321)](fig-2610.03321.png)

**[Information Limits of Low-Rank Approximation Certification](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03321v1)** — A low-rank approximation can need extra matrix-vector products to verify that its error meets a prescribed tolerance. This paper characterizes that certification cost. For a single approximation matrix candidate, it determines the exact dimension-uniform minimax query constant. The verification contribution is the main result for reusing validation responses. One batch supports an entire nested path of candidates without increasing the query budget. Across multiple paths, a concentration bound yields a square-root dependence on the number of paths, and a matching lower bound proves optimality. The procedure verifies different certificate families at costs that scale as the number of matrix dimensions raised to the one-third and two-thirds powers.

![Figure 1 from “Lightweight, Rubric-Guided Trajectory Evaluation for Production AI Agents” (arXiv:2610.03315)](fig-2610.03315.png)

**[Lightweight, Rubric-Guided Trajectory Evaluation for Production AI Agents](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03315v1)** — Production agents generate long trajectories with tool calls, observations, and retries. Evaluating these trajectories repeatedly is expensive. LiteTrajEval is a lightweight architecture for budget-bounded evaluation. It derives domain-specific rule profiles offline and serializes each trajectory under a fixed budget online. A single rubric-guided LLM judge then produces a structured diagnostic report. The validation-methodology contribution is this architecture itself. On public Magentic-One-style and tau-bench-style datasets, LiteTrajEval improves failure-localization alignment with human annotations by up to 35 percentage points over AgentRx. It reduces cost by about six times and evaluation time by more than eight times.

![Figure 1 from “JOVE: Joint Execution and Verification for Resource-Aware LLM Task Graphs” (arXiv:2610.03296)](fig-2610.03296.png)

**[JOVE: Joint Execution and Verification for Resource-Aware LLM Task Graphs](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03296v1)** — Complex reasoning queries can be split into task graphs and distributed across different LLMs. The suitability of an LLM for a subtask is often unknown ahead of time, and execution alone does not reveal if the output is correct. JOVE is an online framework that jointly assigns executor LLMs to subtasks and selects intermediate outputs for paid verification. Verification runs asynchronously and feeds back into future allocations. The verification contribution is this online decision framework itself. It solves a sequence of per-query mixed-integer linear programs to balance spending on execution against learning for later. Across four reasoning benchmarks, JOVE achieves competitive accuracy while reducing average cost and latency by at least 3.17 times.

![Figure 1 from “Coverage You Can Steer: Online Conformal Calibration for RL-Driven Hardware-Aware NAS” (arXiv:2610.03127)](fig-2610.03127.png)

**[Coverage You Can Steer: Online Conformal Calibration for RL-Driven Hardware-Aware NAS](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03127v1)** — Conformal-prediction filters can prune candidates in hardware-aware neural architecture search by guaranteeing a limit on wrongly discarded candidates. The guarantee assumes exchangeability. The surrounding reinforcement-learning loop violates this assumption because the policy's proposals improve as search proceeds. This paper replaces one-shot quantile estimation with online feedback control using Adaptive Conformal Inference variants. The uncertainty-quantification contribution is restoring steerable coverage: dialing a target coverage level delivers it monotonically and reproducibly across arbitrary non-exchangeable sequences. The method tracks every requested level to within about 0.001 while pruning 25 to 50 percent of evaluations at no measured accuracy cost.

![Figure 1 from “Ask, Relax, or Act? Evaluating Actionable Indeterminacy in LLM Preference Reasoning” (arXiv:2610.03102)](fig-2610.03102.png)

**[Ask, Relax, or Act? Evaluating Actionable Indeterminacy in LLM Preference Reasoning](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03102v1)** — An LLM agent can recognize it is uncertain about the right action. Choosing the wrong next step is still possible: asking when action is already justified, or seeking clarification when the constraints must change. This paper formalizes actionable indeterminacy. An agent should act when an action is shared across all admissible preferences, clarify when no action is shared, and propose a constraint repair when a request is infeasible. The validation-methodology contribution is this formalization and a solver-grounded benchmark. Findings show that models often intervene when intervention is not necessary. Making the required content explicit substantially improves fully correct responses.

![Figure 1 from “MOF-VERIFY: A Failure-Aware Agentic Harness for MOF Hypothesis Verification” (arXiv:2610.03056)](fig-2610.03056.png)

**[MOF-VERIFY: A Failure-Aware Agentic Harness for MOF Hypothesis Verification](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03056v1)** — Large language models are used in materials science to verify hypotheses about metal-organic frameworks. The verification pipeline is often unreliable. Structures can appear under different identifiers. Evidence is distributed across heterogeneous sources. Some hypotheses need computation rather than just literature. This paper introduces a diagnostic benchmark with four task families that localize failure modes in knowledge access, evidence acquisition, and reasoning. The verification contribution is MOF-Verify, a failure-aware agentic harness built from those diagnosed failure modes. It targets structural, literature, evidence-sufficiency, and computational bottlenecks before producing a final verdict. It substantially improves verification performance over direct inference and retrieval-based baselines.

**[Cross-Fitting Under Nonregularity: Normality and Inference via Locality](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02944v1)** — Cross-fitting is a standard technique in applied research. Conventional confidence intervals that ignore cross-fold dependence undercover in many applications. This paper exploits a locality condition to show a central limit theorem for a large class of cross-fitting estimators despite the nonregularity. The uncertainty-quantification contribution is the method for estimating the cross-fold correlation and constructing new confidence intervals that attain asymptotically nominal coverage. Simulations with random forests and neural networks show approximately nominal coverage, fixing the undercoverage problem.

![Figure 1 from “Discriminating Fixture Coverage in Agent-Infrastructure Verification Suites” (arXiv:2610.02928)](fig-2610.02928.png)

**[Discriminating Fixture Coverage in Agent-Infrastructure Verification Suites](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02928v1)** — Invariant suites gate agent deployment decisions. The standard validation passes a suite against an implementation believed correct and one believed broken. This paper applies mutation analysis to an invariant suite for an agent state-projection layer. It finds that a mutant removing event-identity deduplication survives every check. The suite is frozen and run against ten adversarial mutants. It kills five. The verification contribution is the analysis of the two distinct failure modes. Three survivors are never activated. Two corrupt internal state that no oracle can observe. The paper proposes a coverage approach by enumerating seven discriminating dimensions. Adding one fixture per uncovered dimension kills all five survivors.

![Figure 2 from “Revealing Epistemic Uncertainty in MLLMs via Causal-Invariant Masking” (arXiv:2610.02887)](fig-2610.02887.png)

**[Revealing Epistemic Uncertainty in MLLMs via Causal-Invariant Masking](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02887v1)** — Multimodal large language models suffer from hallucinations. Existing uncertainty-quantification approaches struggle to detect uncertainty caused by superficial associations. The problem is a bias toward aleatoric uncertainty from data ambiguity while overlooking epistemic uncertainty from model limitations. This paper proposes Causal-Invariant Masking to decompose these two types. It measures the semantic shift between original predictions and those conditioned on a causally-focused view. The uncertainty-quantification contribution is the Semantic Divergence metric, which converges to the variance of the model's sensitivity to non-causal correlations. The paper also introduces Expected Embedding Drift as a fast geometric proxy that achieves nearly 50 percent speedup with comparable performance.

![Figure 1 from “Bounded Reachability & Jailbreak Detection via Contraction-Constrained State Space Models” (arXiv:2610.02853)](fig-2610.02853.png)

**[Bounded Reachability & Jailbreak Detection via Contraction-Constrained State Space Models](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02853v1)** — Safety heads are lightweight classifiers attached to language models to flag harmful inputs. Their formal robustness properties are largely unexplored. This paper asks when a state-space-model-based safety head can be certified to produce the same prediction for all inputs within a bounded perturbation. The verification contribution is the proof that the answer depends on one condition: the largest absolute row sum of the state transition matrix must be less than one. When this contraction condition holds, exact interval bound propagation certification is possible. When it fails, the output interval grows exponentially. Enforcing contraction with a hinge penalty improves the certified fraction from 41 percent to 59 percent on toxic comment data.

![Figure 3 from “MLCommons Jailbreak Benchmark v1.0” (arXiv:2610.02827)](fig-2610.02827.png)

**[MLCommons Jailbreak Benchmark v1.0](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02827v1)** — Jailbreak attacks are prompts crafted to bypass safety safeguards. This benchmark provides an end-to-end methodology for evaluating LLM robustness to single-turn jailbreak attacks. It combines criteria-driven system and attack selection, paired baseline and adversarial evaluation, human annotation, automated evaluator calibration, and scoring. The validation-methodology contribution is the standardized methodology itself. The main metric is the Resilience Gap: the change in safety performance between baseline and adversarial conditions. The average gap across eight open-weight systems was 7.57 percent. The benchmark establishes a reproducible foundation for comparative jailbreak evaluation.

![Figure 1 from “Law And Order: Tax Law Autoformalization” (arXiv:2610.02792)](fig-2610.02792.png)

**[Law And Order: Tax Law Autoformalization](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02792v1)** — Legal systems are implemented through software. Translating legal texts into accurate symbolic representations at scale is an open problem. This paper studies the problem through tax law, where forms and instructions define large computational structures. It proposes Law&Order, a neuro-symbolic framework that combines LLM synthesis with cell-level verification and iterative localized error repair. The verification contribution is the formal verification loop. It uses human-written tax returns as a specification. The framework achieves 100 percent cell-level and form-level accuracy on 51 held-out returns. The most advanced LLM alone achieves only 66 percent.

![Figure 1 from “On the Chain-of-Thought Monitorability of Looped Language Models” (arXiv:2610.02741)](fig-2610.02741.png)

**[On the Chain-of-Thought Monitorability of Looped Language Models](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02741v1)** — Chain-of-thought monitoring is a promising approach for detecting undesirable model behavior. Looped language models repeatedly apply shared transformer layers. The effect of looped architectures on monitorability is unexplored. This paper provides the first systematic evaluation. It studies varying loop depth and compares looped models to non-looped models. The validation-methodology contribution is the evaluation itself. Task-dependent reductions in monitorability under stress tests are observed on specific tasks. The declines are not fully explained by task difficulty or verification pass rate. Looped transformer architecture alone does not necessarily imply lower monitorability.

![Figure 1 from “Test-time Calibration Learning for Large Language Model Reasoning” (arXiv:2610.02695)](fig-2610.02695.png)

**[Test-time Calibration Learning for Large Language Model Reasoning](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02695v1)** — A reliable LLM must express confidence that matches its actual probability of being correct. Existing calibration-learning methods rely on ground-truth labels. This is a problem for test-time settings where labels are unavailable and calibration needs to adapt to new tasks. This paper proposes Test-Time Calibration Learning, a label-free framework. It derives self-supervision signals for both correctness and calibration from multiple model-generated responses. The uncertainty-quantification contribution is this framework and its theoretical bounds. On base models, it achieves an average relative accuracy improvement of over 40 percent and a reduction in expected calibration error of over 70 percent across eight benchmarks.

![Figure 1 from “Mind the Refinement Gap: When Safe High-Level Robot Plans Produce Unsafe Executions” (arXiv:2610.02662)](fig-2610.02662.png)

**[Mind the Refinement Gap: When Safe High-Level Robot Plans Produce Unsafe Executions](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02662v1)** — Language-enabled robot systems combine semantic-graph planning with temporal-logic safety monitors. This paper investigates a hidden assumption in these systems: whether the high-level action sequence checked by the monitor actually represents the navigation and effects that happen during execution. It audits this assumption in RoboGuard by comparing its verdict on a surface plan with its verdict on a graph-refined trace under the same specification. The validation-methodology contribution is the finding of a clear discrepancy. All twelve targeted abstraction cases exhibit the predicted surface-versus-refined discrepancy. All sixteen controls behave as expected. The paper motivates graph-based trace refinement as a lightweight mitigation.

![Figure 1 from “Online Verification of Language Model Responses Under Cost Constraints” (arXiv:2610.02632)](fig-2610.02632.png)

**[Online Verification of Language Model Responses Under Cost Constraints](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02632v1)** — Verifying LLM outputs by querying a costly ground-truth oracle at every step is impractical. Prior work uses a single fixed weak verifier to decide when to query the strong verifier. This paper argues that a single weak verifier may not perform consistently as the subject matter changes. It introduces Online Multi-Verifier Verification (OMVV). OMVV maintains a pool of candidate weak verifiers with different costs and performance. The verification contribution is the algorithm itself. It adaptively routes each decision to a verifier using an online score combiner and exponential-weights routing. OMVV provides distribution-free guarantees and achieves a regret that grows sublinearly: its error rate stays closer to the best fixed verifier's rate over time. It achieves higher accuracy at lower cost than any single fixed verifier.
