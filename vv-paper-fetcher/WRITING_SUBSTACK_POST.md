# How to write the weekly Substack post from a digest report

This describes how to turn a weekly `reports/<date>.md` digest (generated by this
pipeline) into a Substack post. Follow it exactly — it reflects several rounds of
correction from the user, not just a first-draft guess.

## 1. Source material

The input is `reports/<date>.md`: a "Top papers" list (usually 20, ranked mostly by
author reputation/h-index, not novelty) plus a shorter "Honorable mentions" list at
the end. Each top-paper entry has a title, arXiv/HF link, authors, venue, a
reputation line, an auto-generated one-line summary, and auto-assigned tags
(`verification`, `evals`, `agents`, `uncertainty-quantification`, etc.).

**Do not trust the tags or the one-line summaries as-is.** They come from an
automated LLM triage pass and are frequently wrong about what a paper is actually
about — a plain capability benchmark can get tagged `evals`/`verification` even
when it has no real verification-methodology content.

## 2. Filter to genuine VV/UQ content — go over every paper, not just the top 10

The post only covers papers whose **core contribution** is genuinely one of:

- **Verification** — formally or empirically checking that an AI system's outputs
  or claims are correct/consistent (proof checkers, formal verifiers, hallucination
  detectors that check groundedness, consistency-checking protocols).
- **Validation** — a methodology for checking an AI system does what it's supposed
  to do, where the *methodology itself* is the contribution (e.g. a benchmark whose
  grading method is validated against human experts, a stress test that reveals a
  real robustness gap, an audit tool). A benchmark that only measures a capability
  with no real validation-methodology angle does **not** qualify.
- **Uncertainty quantification (UQ)** — calibration, confidence estimates, posterior/
  distributional reasoning about model outputs, or drift-aware confidence.

**Drop everything else** — generic agent-capability benchmarks, position/survey
papers about agent architectures, applications that only touch V/V/UQ tangentially
(e.g. a chip-design tool that happens to help verification elsewhere, an XAI system
whose internal consistency check is a minor component). Go over **all** papers in
the report — top 20 plus honorable mentions — not just the top-ranked ones. The
report's own ranking is by author reputation, which has nothing to do with V/V/UQ
relevance; strong candidates have shown up in the honorable-mentions section before.

Expect roughly 25–35% of the full paper list to survive this filter. The rest gets
dropped, even prominent top-10 entries.

### How to do this filtering in practice

Fetch the real abstract for every paper (WebFetch on the arXiv abstract page,
`http://arxiv.org/abs/XXXX.XXXXXvN`, or the HF Papers page for HF-only entries) —
don't rely on the digest's one-liner. Because this means dozens of fetches, delegate
it to a background `general-purpose` Agent rather than doing it inline: give it the
full paper list (title, link, digest's one-line summary, tags) and ask for, per
paper, a KEEP/DROP verdict against the strict definition above, a one-line rationale,
and — for KEEP only — a fuller 4–6 sentence plain-English summary of the actual
method and result (this is the raw material for the paragraph you'll write). Tell it
to be strict and honest: a paper tagged "evals" or "verification" by the pipeline
should still get DROP if its real core contribution is just a capability benchmark.

## 3. Audience — write for people who use AI, not people who build it

The reader is not an academic, not a computer scientist, and not an AI researcher.
The reader is someone who uses AI tools day to day and has no formal training in
the field. **The reader is smart, curious, and comfortable with abstract ideas —
the reader is missing the field-specific vocabulary, not the general intelligence
to follow a real explanation.** Write for that reader, not for a child. Getting
this balance wrong in either direction is a real failure mode: unexplained jargon
loses the reader, but a paragraph so simplified it says nothing new also loses the
reader, just later and more insultingly.

- Never use an unexplained technical term. Terms like "posterior," "Bayesian,"
  "SMT solver," "embedding space," "Gaussian mixture model," "complexity class NP,"
  "logit," or a model-architecture acronym (GNN, ViT, GRU) need a short, precise
  gloss the first time they appear in a paragraph — enough for the reader to keep
  following the real explanation, not a full detour into the underlying theory.
- A gloss replaces a jargon word with a plain-language phrase carrying the same
  amount of information — it does not swap a specific claim for a vague one. "A
  compact proof that lets a checker confirm the claims agree, without re-deriving
  them from scratch" is a gloss: it keeps the actual mechanism. "A short proof,"
  with no sense of what the proof contains or why it works, is not a gloss — it's
  a deletion wearing a gloss's clothes.
- Skip beginner-level illustrative examples that explain a concept the reader
  already has (what "probability of rain" means, what a benchmark is in the most
  generic sense). Only reach for an example when it clarifies the specific
  mechanism a paper introduces — not the general domain the paper starts from.
- Keep the real technical content: specific numbers, the actual name of a method,
  and a genuine account of how the method works, not just the problem it claims to
  solve. Simplifying the sentence structure is required; simplifying away the
  substance is not.
- This rule sits on top of the ASD-STE100 rules below, not in place of them:
  simple sentences carrying real ideas, not simplified ideas in simple sentences.

## 4. Write the post in ASD-STE100 controlled English

The user wants the post written in **ASD-STE100** (Simplified Technical English), a
controlled-language standard. Apply its rules throughout:

- Short sentences — aim for ~20 words or fewer.
- One action per sentence. Split any sentence joining two independent actions with
  "and" into two sentences.
- Active voice only, simple verb tenses (simple present, simple past, imperative,
  "can"/"must" for permission/obligation). No complex tenses (present perfect,
  future continuous, etc.).
- No idioms, metaphors, or figurative language ("closer to a smoke detector than a
  fire investigator" — do not write things like this).
- No vague pronouns. When a sentence would need "it" and the antecedent isn't
  perfectly obvious, repeat the noun instead ("This paper builds a tool named
  UniProbe. UniProbe reads..." not "...UniProbe. It reads...").
- Avoid generic AI-writing filler: no "in today's rapidly evolving landscape," no
  "game-changer"/"cutting-edge"/"revolutionary," no "here's why this matters" as a
  standalone bridge, no engagement-bait closing question, no generic wrap-up
  paragraph at the end. (These bans come from the `ecc:article-writing` skill —
  invoke it for the full rule set if unsure.)

## 5. Structure

1. **Title** — short, concrete, and specific to what actually survived the filter
   (e.g. "Nine Ways to Check If an AI System Is Correct"), not a generic "Weekly AI
   Roundup." Update the number in the title to match however many papers survived
   step 2 — it will vary week to week.
2. **Intro paragraph** — a concrete rundown of what each surviving paper actually
   does, in one or two short sentences per paper. Do **not** open by restating the
   newsletter's overall premise ("how do you know an AI system tells the truth?") —
   that framing is redundant given the whole post is already about that; the intro
   needs to be a preview of *contents*, not a repeat of the *premise*.
3. **One paragraph per surviving paper**, in the source report's ranked order, each:
   - Named and linked with `**[Title](url)** — ` at the start of the paragraph. The
     URL must be copied exactly from the source report — verify this with a grep
     comparison after writing (see Verification below).
   - Leads with the concrete problem or method, not "this paper presents...".
   - Contains one explicit sentence naming the paper's specific V/V/UQ
     contribution — e.g. "The verification contribution is..." / "The specific
     uncertainty-quantification contribution is this: ..." / "The
     validation-methodology contribution is a warning result." Don't leave the
     V/V/UQ angle merely implied — say it directly.
   - **Never mentions author names.** Refer to "this paper," "the benchmark," or
     the method's own name (e.g. "ERRLESS," "UniProbe," "DDA-UQ," "J-Access") as the
     grammatical subject instead of listing authors. This was an explicit,
     repeated correction from the user — do not reintroduce author names, and do
     not fall back on a templated "X, Y, and Z, and other authors build..." sentence
     pattern (also explicitly banned — it read as mail-merged).
   - Roughly 80–200 words.
4. No closing section — end on the last paper's paragraph.

## 6. Output file

Save to `reports/<date>-substack-post-ste100.md` in the repo (not just an Artifact —
the user wants a real file to paste into Substack's editor). Use plain markdown, no
frontmatter.

## 7. Verification before calling it done

- Extract every arXiv/HF link used in the post and diff it against the links for
  the same papers in the source `reports/<date>.md` — they must match exactly,
  including the version suffix (`v1`, `v2`, ...). A quick way:
  `grep -oE 'arxiv\.org/abs/[0-9]+\.[0-9]+v[0-9]+' <post file>` and compare.
- Grep the finished post for author-name-shaped patterns and confirm none slipped
  in.
- Re-read for the banned-phrase list in step 4.
- Re-read for any technical term used without a plain-language gloss on first use
  (e.g. "posterior," "SMT," "embedding space," model-architecture acronyms) — a
  reader with no AI background must be able to follow every sentence.
- Re-read for the opposite failure: a gloss or example so simplified it drops the
  actual mechanism, or a beginner-level example explaining a concept the reader
  already has. If a paragraph could apply to almost any paper in the field, it has
  been over-simplified — tighten it back up around the paper's specific method.
- Confirm paragraph count equals 1 intro + (number of KEEP papers).
